## What is Cabruca?

**Cabruca** is a traditional agroforestry system from the southern region of Bahia, Brazil. It consists of cultivating **cocoa** (*Theobroma cacao*) under the shade of native trees from the Atlantic Forest. Instead of clearing the entire area, the producer performs a **thinning** (cutting only the understory and some smaller trees), preserving tall trees to provide shade for the cocoa.

The name comes from the verb **"cabrucar"**, which means precisely this process of clearing the brush without felling the large trees.

## Origin

It emerged in the **18th century** (around 1746), in the captaincy of Ilhéus, when colonizers realized that cocoa developed well under the shade of the forest, imitating its natural habitat in the Amazon.

## Main Characteristics

| **Main crop**           | Cocoa                                                                   |

| **Region**              | Southern Bahia (Ilhéus, Itabuna, Una, etc.)                             |
| **Biome**               | Atlantic Forest                                                         |

## Environmental Importance

Cabruca is considered one of the most harmonious ways to reconcile agricultural production and environmental conservation. Among its benefits:

- **Biodiversity conservation**: it shelters species of fauna and flora from the Atlantic Forest, including trees threatened with extinction.
- **Forest connectivity**: it functions as an ecological corridor between forest fragments.
- **Carbon sequestration**: estimated at around **170 tons of carbon per hectare**.
- **Soil and water protection**: it reduces erosion and maintains springs.
- **Microclimate**: it keeps temperature and humidity more stable.

Research has already recorded more than **40 tree species** in just 2.6 hectares of cabruca, with a Shannon diversity index of **3.35** — a fairly high value.

## Economic Importance

Cabruca cocoa has potential for **valorization in the premium market**, due to its sustainable character and differentiated quality. Bahia was once the world's largest cocoa producer, and Cabruca is a central part of that history.

## Current Challenges

Despite its benefits, cabruca faces serious problems:

1. **Low productivity**: the average is only **200 kg/ha**, when it could reach **1,000 kg/ha** with proper management.
2. **Lack of technical assistance**: many producers do not know how to correctly manage shade trees and cocoa trees.
3. **Pressure for conversion**: some producers replace cabruca with full-sun cultivation or clear land to sell timber.
4. **Absence of payment for environmental services**: the producer receives nothing for the conservation they maintain.
5. **Traceability requirements**: regulations such as the European zero-deforestation regulation (EUDR) pressure small producers without organization.

## Public Policies and Recent Initiatives

- **Draft laws** (such as PL 4995/2009 and PL 4107/2025) seek to create a **National Cabruca Policy**, with credit, technical assistance, and payment for environmental services.
- In **2026**, **FAO** and **CEPLAC** launched a cooperation project to:
  - Restore **12,000 hectares** of cabruca;
  - Improve the management of more than **203,000 hectares**;
  - **Triple** average productivity;
  - Increase the income of beneficiary families by **30%**.
- The most recent proposal provides for a minimum payment of **1 arroba of cocoa (15 kg) per hectare/year** as environmental compensation.

## Summary

Cabruca is a unique system in the world: **it produces cocoa and conserves forest at the same time**. It is historical heritage, environmental patrimony, and a source of income for thousands of families in southern Bahia. Its future depends on **technology, market valorization, and public policies** that recognize its environmental value.

### 🌍 What is GIAHS?

The full name of GIAHS is **"Globally Important Agricultural Heritage Systems"**, a program of the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) launched at the 2002 World Summit on Sustainable Development and formally incorporated as an FAO institutional project in 2015.

**Its nature is**: it is not about protecting "relics," but about dynamically identifying and supporting those land-use systems formed over generations that possess rich agricultural biodiversity, traditional knowledge, and unique landscapes, and that still maintain production and livelihood capacities. These systems demonstrate how humans can coexist harmoniously with nature while ensuring food security.

**Current scale**: as of 2025, FAO has designated **104 GIAHS sites globally**, distributed across 29 countries.

**Designation and management process**: countries or interested parties submit proposals, which are evaluated by an independent Scientific Advisory Group (SAG). The proposal must demonstrate the system's unique value in terms of food and livelihood security, agricultural biodiversity, traditional knowledge, culture, and landscapes. After designation, a "dynamic conservation plan" must be implemented to ensure the system continues to develop.

### 🌱 How does the FAO project in southern Bahia work in practice?

This project was launched in **April 2026**, with an expected duration until **2032**. Its central logic is: **use "Cabruca" to protect the forest and, through the forest, generate income.**

**Operational mechanism**: FAO provides technical support, GEF (Global Environment Facility) provides financing, the Brazilian government agency CEPLAC is responsible for execution, and the local social institution Sitawi is the operational partner. The project directly serves **3,000 cocoa producers**, organized through three associations (CIAPRA, CIMA, CDS-LS), with the requirement that at least half of the beneficiaries be **women and young people**.

**How producers participate specifically**:

1. **Continuous technical assistance**: technicians teach producers how to properly manage shade trees and cocoa trees to increase productivity. The goal is to raise the average yield from about **200 kg/hectare** to **1,000 kg/hectare** or more, that is, **triple** it.

2. **Green financing**: through the **Sustainable CRA** model (Certificate of Agribusiness Receivables, a sustainable agricultural financing instrument), producers obtain working capital, using their environmental and social practices as collateral.

3. **Access to premium markets**: the project establishes a connection with the fine cocoa market, where Cabruca cocoa, due to its quality and environmental history, can obtain **preferential prices**.

**Three key tools**:

- **Blockchain Traceability**: record the entire process of cocoa from the field to the chocolate bar, meeting the demands of European buyers regarding origin and deforestation.
- **Cocoa School**: a permanent training center that trains producers and technicians.
- **Territorial Intelligence Center (CIT)**: use remote sensing technology and real-time monitoring to track changes in landscape and biodiversity.

**Main result targets**:

- **Environmental**: restore **12,000 hectares** of Cabruca cocoa; improve the management of more than **203,000 hectares** of protected areas; reduce **3.72 million tons** of greenhouse gases.
- **Economic**: increase producers' income by **30%**; triple productivity.
- **Social**: serve 3,000 producer families, with at least half being women and young people.

### 💡 The difference between the two

It is important to distinguish: **GIAHS is a "seal" of recognition and a broad conceptual program**; while **the project in southern Bahia is a specific action** that applies the idea of "productive conservation" on the ground. If Cabruca in the future becomes a GIAHS site, the region will gain greater visibility and financing opportunities; but even if it has not yet obtained the designation, this project is already operating with its own financing and targets.

### 📌 What Green Goods is, in practice

Green Goods is a **"local-first" regenerative compliance and impact reporting platform**, built by the Greenpill Dev Guild. It works as a **PWA (Progressive Web App)** designed to function under field conditions: offline-first, on mid-range Android devices, with support in English, Portuguese, and Spanish.

The central workflow is the **MDR (Media → Details → Review)**:

1. **Media:** the field worker takes photos or videos of the work performed.
2. **Details:** fills out a form specific to the action (e.g., number of trees planted, species, geographic location).
3. **Review:** reviews and submits. The work enters a community review queue.

The verified work is then transformed into **on-chain attestations (EAS)** and aggregated into **Hypercerts** (tokenized impact certificates). This allows the impact to be tracked and, theoretically, funded by donors or funds.

### 🌱 Direct connection with the Brazilian context

Green Goods already has an active presence in Brazil and is targeting exactly the kind of work relevant to GIAHS.

- **Pilot in Brazil:** In August 2024, a **pilot event was held in Rio Claro, SP**, where participants used the app to document the removal of an invasive species (*Leucaena leucocephala*) and the planting of native trees.
- **Agroforestry Domain:** The platform already has a specific action domain for **Agroforestry**, with impact metrics such as "trees planted, species diversity, canopy cover, harvest yield." This is directly applicable to Cabruca.
- **Language and context:** Full Portuguese support and the design for low connectivity show that it was designed for realities like that of Bahia.

### ⚖️ How Green Goods aligns with GIAHS principles

For a tool to be useful in a GIAHS site, it needs to support **"dynamic conservation"** — that is, keeping the system alive and productive, not freezing it. See how Green Goods fits:

| GIAHS Principle                                    | How Green Goods can help                                                                                                                                                        |
|:-------------------------------------------------- |:------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Monitoring of agricultural biodiversity**        | The tool allows recording observations of flora and fauna, species counts, and canopy cover, creating a continuous and verifiable database.                                     |
| **Economic valorization of products and services** | By transforming conservation work into Hypercerts, Green Goods creates a digital "backing" that can be used to access premium markets or payments for environmental services.   |
| **Empowerment of local communities**               | Verification is done by "neighbors verifying neighbors," through local stewards, and not by a distant authority. This aligns with the community governance that GIAHS values.   |
| **Traditional knowledge**                          | The app allows the producer's knowledge about their land (e.g., "this tree is good for shade") to be recorded as part of the impact data, giving visibility to local knowledge. |

### ⚠️ Limitations and what Green Goods *does not* do

It is crucial to understand what the tool is not.

1. **It is not a FAO seal.** GIAHS recognition is a formal application and evaluation process conducted by FAO. Green Goods is a **monitoring and evidence** tool that could *feed* this process, but it does not grant the title.
2. **It does not replace formal governance.** GIAHS requires governance structures, conservation plans, and complex institutional agreements. Green Goods is a **coordination and recording** layer within this ecosystem, not the entire structure.
3. **Focus on evidence, not technical assistance.** The app records what was done. It does not teach the producer how to manage cabruca nor does it provide credit directly. It creates the **verifiable record** that can be used to access credit or assistance.

### 💎 Conclusion

Green Goods is a promising tool for the GIAHS ecosystem, especially for the Cabruca context in Bahia. It solves a practical problem: **how to document and verify, cheaply and under field conditions, the work of productive conservation that GIAHS promotes.**

It fits as a **community monitoring and traceability layer** within a GIAHS initiative. If Cabruca is ever recognized as a GIAHS site, tools like Green Goods would be exactly the kind of infrastructure needed to demonstrate that the system is being maintained "dynamically" and to connect producers to new financing flows.

### 🌱 The technical basis is solid: Green Goods' entry point is exactly planting traceability

The core function of Green Goods is the "MDR workflow (Media→Details→Review)": field workers take photos, fill out forms, and the reviewed information is encapsulated on the Ethereum blockchain as immutable "attestations." In the "Agroforestry" domain, the metrics it supports directly include: **number of trees planted, species diversity, canopy cover, harvest yield**.

Applying this to the scenario you mentioned: when registering cocoa seedlings at the time of planting, it is possible to record in a structured way **the variety of the seedlings, place of origin, quantity, planting plots, and survival status**. This is precisely the kind of baseline evidence material that a GIAHS application needs to provide.

### 🏭 The Biofábrica side: there is already a physical basis for traceability

The Cocoa Biofábrica of Bahia is currently **the largest cocoa seedling multiplication center in the world**, with 20 nurseries, and an annual production capacity of up to 12 million seedlings. Its seedling production system already has rigorous standards: selection of genetic material, substrate control, rooting techniques, acclimatization, and other key processes are clearly emphasized in training.

What does this mean? **The source of seedlings is controllable and production standards are clear.** Green Goods does not need to "create from scratch" the origin of the data; it connects directly to the batch output information already existing at the Biofábrica, becoming a lightweight traceability layer "nursery → planting."

### 📜 Alignment with the core requirements of GIAHS

The GIAHS application requires demonstrating five main items, and planting traceability is directly applicable to **at least three of them**:

| GIAHS evaluation criterion                       | Support from the traceability solution                                                                                                                                 |
|:------------------------------------------------ |:---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Food and livelihood security**                 | Traceability of seedling origin helps ensure the quality and productivity of cocoa, which is concrete support for farmers' livelihood security                         |
| **Agrobiodiversity**                             | It is possible to record **which species** were planted, **where** they were planted, generating spatially explicit biodiversity distribution data                     |
| **Traditional knowledge and technology systems** | Recording the **Cabruca planting context** (under which native trees, with what spacing) can be structured, helping demonstrate the continuity of "Cabruca technology" |

Especially regarding the third point, GIAHS documents emphasize the need to explain "natural resource management systems that support agricultural activities." When Cabruca plantings are digitized and traced, the traditional logic of "planting cocoa under shade trees" becomes **verifiable and auditable operation records**, which is far more persuasive than a textual description alone.

### ⚠️ Limitations worth noting

Green Goods traceability solves the problem of "**proof of origin**," but GIAHS requirements go further. GIAHS also examines:

- **Traditional knowledge system**: how shade trees are selected, what the planting rules are, how this is transmitted between generations. Currently, Green Goods is more focused on outcome entry, not knowledge documentation.
- **Culture and social organization**: festivals, community cooperation systems, values. This is a dimension that Green Goods cannot currently cover.
- **Landscape characteristics**: the beauty and uniqueness of the Cabruca landscape require field assessment and remote sensing analysis, not just planting point records.

### 💎 Conclusion

**Using Green Goods to trace the chain "Biofábrica → nursery → Cabruca planting" is a technically feasible starting point and highly relevant to GIAHS criteria.** It transforms planting records that previously existed only on paper or Excel into **immutable and spatially anchored evidence on the blockchain**, which has direct practical value both for GIAHS review and for the subsequent sale of premium cocoa with "origin traceability."

If this data chain is truly established, it could become one of the most solid "production-level evidence layers" of the GIAHS application for Cabruca.
